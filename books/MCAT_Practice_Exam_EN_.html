Passage 1 (Questions 1–5): The Bicarbonate Buffer System

The bicarbonate buffer system is the most important extracellular buffer in human blood plasma, keeping blood pH within the narrow physiological range of 7.35–7.45. It involves an equilibrium among dissolved carbon dioxide (CO₂), carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), and bicarbonate ion (HCO₃⁻):

CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

The apparent pKa of carbonic acid dissociation is about 6.1. By the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation:

pH = pKa + log([HCO₃⁻] / [CO₂]dissolved)

Dissolved CO₂ concentration can be estimated from PCO₂ via Henry's law: [CO₂]dissolved = 0.03 × PCO₂ (mmol/L, with PCO₂ in mmHg). Under normal physiological conditions, the [HCO₃⁻]:[CO₂] ratio is about 20:1. The bicarbonate system remains an effective buffer even far from its own pKa (physiological pH 7.4) because it is an "open system" — the lungs continuously adjust CO₂ elimination through respiration, and the kidneys adjust HCO₃⁻ reabsorption and excretion.

Passage 2 (Questions 6–10): Fluid Dynamics in Blood Vessels

Blood flow through vessels can be approximated using principles of fluid dynamics. Under laminar flow, Poiseuille's Law gives the volumetric flow rate Q as:

Q = (πΔPr⁴) / (8ηL)

where ΔP is the pressure difference, r is vessel radius, η is blood viscosity, and L is vessel length. Flow is extremely sensitive to radius, scaling with r⁴.

Additionally, by the continuity equation A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ and Bernoulli's principle, a narrowing in a vessel decreases cross-sectional area, increasing flow velocity; the increased velocity in turn lowers pressure at that site. When velocity becomes too high or vessel geometry is irregular, laminar flow may transition to turbulent flow — typically when the Reynolds number (Re = ρvd/η) exceeds roughly 2000 — producing an audible bruit.

Passage 3 (Questions 11–15): Organic Reaction Mechanisms (SN1/SN2/E1/E2)

The mechanism of nucleophilic substitution and elimination reactions of alkyl halides (SN1, SN2, E1, E2) is influenced by substrate structure, the strength of the nucleophile/base, solvent polarity, and other factors. SN2 is a single concerted step in which the nucleophile attacks from the side opposite the leaving group; the rate depends on both nucleophile and substrate concentration (second-order kinetics), it is most favorable for primary alkyl halides with little steric hindrance, and it proceeds with inversion of configuration (Walden inversion). SN1 instead proceeds through a planar carbocation intermediate; the rate depends only on substrate concentration (first-order kinetics), it is most favorable for tertiary alkyl halides, and it typically yields racemic (or nearly racemic) product.

Passage 4 (Questions 16–20): Thermochemistry and Calorimetry

Calorimetry measures the heat released or absorbed during a chemical reaction or physical process. At constant pressure, the heat released by a reaction equals the negative of the reaction's enthalpy change ΔH (exothermic reactions have ΔH<0). By Hess's Law, the total enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of pathway and depends only on the initial and final states, so the enthalpy change of a target reaction can be calculated by combining the enthalpy changes of known reactions.

Passage 5 (Questions 21–25): Galvanic Cells and Electrochemistry

A galvanic cell uses a spontaneous redox reaction to convert chemical energy into electrical energy. The cell consists of two half-cells, each with an electrode immersed in its corresponding electrolyte solution; the two half-cells are connected by a salt bridge to maintain charge balance, and an external circuit connects the two electrodes via wire to complete the current loop. Cell potential (E°cell) can be calculated from standard reduction potentials: E°cell = E°(cathode, reduction) − E°(anode, reduction). Electrons flow through the external circuit from the anode (where oxidation occurs) to the cathode (where reduction occurs).

The table below lists standard reduction potentials for several half-reactions:

Passage 6 (Questions 26–30): Mechanical Waves and the Doppler Effect

Sound is a longitudinal wave whose propagation speed depends on the properties of the medium (density, elastic modulus), traveling at roughly 343 m/s in air at 20°C. When there is relative motion between a sound source and an observer, the frequency perceived by the observer differs from the actual frequency emitted by the source — a phenomenon known as the Doppler effect. When a source approaches an observer, the perceived frequency increases; when the source recedes, the perceived frequency decreases. The general Doppler formula is:

f_observed = f_source × (v ± v_observer)/(v ∓ v_source)

where v is the speed of sound, and v_observer and v_source are the velocities of the observer and source relative to the medium (signs depend on direction of relative motion). Additionally, when a source's driving frequency matches the natural frequency of a medium or object, resonance occurs, and amplitude increases dramatically — a principle widely applied in musical instrument design and medical ultrasound imaging equipment.

  2. Higher than 700 Hz
  3. Exactly 700 Hz

Show Answer & Explanation
Answer: B
When a source (ambulance) approaches an observer (pedestrian), the sound waves are "compressed" in the direction of travel, shortening the wavelength, so the observer perceives a frequency higher than the source's natural frequency — the classic Doppler effect (an ambulance siren sounds higher-pitched as it approaches).

Passage 7 (Questions 31–35): Separation and Purification Techniques

Organic chemistry experiments commonly use several techniques to separate and purify mixtures of compounds. Extraction exploits differences in a target compound's solubility between two immiscible solvents (typically an aqueous phase and an organic phase), using a separatory funnel to partition it between phases; adjusting the aqueous phase's pH can selectively convert acidic or basic organic compounds into charged salt forms that remain in the aqueous phase, enabling separation of acidic, basic, and neutral compounds. Distillation exploits differences in boiling point: simple distillation works for mixtures with a large boiling-point difference (typically >25°C), while fractional distillation, using repeated vapor-liquid equilibrations, is better suited to separating compounds with close boiling points. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) separates and identifies compounds based on differences in their partition coefficients between a stationary phase (e.g., silica gel, polar) and a mobile phase (eluent); less polar compounds typically travel farther with the mobile phase, giving a larger retention factor (Rf value).

Passage 8 (Questions 36–40): Radioactive Decay and Nuclear Physics

Radioactive isotopes spontaneously undergo nuclear decay to form more stable nuclides. Major decay modes include alpha decay (emission of a helium nucleus; mass number decreases by 4, atomic number decreases by 2), beta-minus decay (a neutron converts to a proton with emission of an electron; mass number unchanged, atomic number increases by 1), and gamma decay (emission of a high-energy photon; both mass number and atomic number unchanged, usually accompanying another decay mode to release excess energy). Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics, characterized by a constant half-life (t½) — the time required for the amount of a radioactive nuclide in a sample to decrease to half its initial value, independent of the remaining amount. The relationship between remaining quantity and time is:

N(t) = N₀ × (1/2)^(t/t½)

Show Answer & Explanation

Passage 2 (Questions 7–12): The Effective Altruism Debate

In recent years, the "effective altruism" movement has argued that charitable giving should be guided by rigorous cost-benefit analysis: donors should direct resources toward whichever programs can save the most lives or alleviate the most suffering at the lowest cost, rather than basing donation decisions purely on emotional proximity or geographic connection. Passage 6 (Questions 30–34): Authorial Intent and the Interpretation of Texts

Literary theorists have long debated whether a text's meaning is fixed by its author's intended meaning, or whether meaning is instead something readers actively generate through their own encounter with the text — a debate sometimes framed as authorial intent versus reader-response approaches to interpretation. Passage 8 (Questions 40–44): The Moral Legitimacy of Civil Disobedience

Civil disobedience — the deliberate, public, and typically nonviolent violation of a law in order to protest its injustice or the injustice of a broader policy — occupies an uneasy position within democratic political theory. On one hand, it involves a conscious refusal to comply with duly enacted law; on the other, its practitioners typically frame their actions not as a rejection of the rule of law as such, but as an appeal to a society's own deeper legal and moral principles against a particular law that fails to live up to them. This framing is central to how civil disobedience has traditionally been distinguished, at least in theory, from ordinary lawbreaking: the civil disobedient generally accepts the legitimacy of the legal system as a whole and often willingly accepts legal punishment for the violation, precisely in order to demonstrate the sincerity and non-self-interested character of the protest.

This account, however, raises a further question: under what conditions is civil disobedience morally justified, given that it does involve knowingly breaking the law? Most theorists agree that some limiting conditions are necessary — that the injustice being protested must be serious, that legal channels for redress must have been exhausted or be plausibly unavailable, and that the action must be conducted openly and nonviolently, in a manner that signals appeal to conscience rather than coercion. Yet each of these conditions is itself contestable: how serious must an injustice be, and who is positioned to judge that; when have legal channels truly been "exhausted"; and does a strict nonviolence requirement risk holding protest movements to a standard that structural power imbalances make unrealistic in practice?

A further complication arises when a movement's use of civil disobedience succeeds not merely in drawing sympathetic attention but in materially disrupting ordinary social or economic life — through prolonged blockades, for instance. Some theorists argue that such disruption, even if nonviolent, changes the moral calculus, since it may impose costs on bystanders who bear no responsibility for the injustice being protested; others respond that disruption of ordinary life has often been historically necessary to make an otherwise-ignorable protest impossible to ignore, and that demanding protest remain costless to bystanders effectively grants those bystanders — and by extension the status quo — an unearned veto over dissent.

Passage 9 (Questions 45–49): The Linguistic Relativity Debate

Does language shape the way its speakers think, or even constrain which concepts they are capable of forming? This question is known as linguistic relativity. Its strong version — sometimes called the extreme form of the "Sapir-Whorf hypothesis" — holds that language does not merely influence but substantially determines the cognitive structures of its speakers: if a given language lacks the vocabulary or grammatical category to express a certain concept, its speakers will find it difficult, or even impossible, to form that concept at all. This strong version held considerable influence in the mid-twentieth century but has since been largely abandoned by mainstream linguistics, both for lack of rigorous empirical support and because it faces a logical difficulty: after all, the very fact that translators can render unfamiliar concepts into and out of different languages already suggests that language does not constitute an insurmountable cognitive prison.

A more moderate version, however, has regained attention in recent years, supported by a number of rigorous empirical studies: language may not "determine" the boundaries of thought, but it may influence the default way information is processed, attended to, or remembered in certain cognitive tasks. For example, some languages require speakers to specify absolute cardinal direction in nearly every sentence (e.g., "the cup to the east" rather than "the cup on your left"); speakers of such languages tend to outperform speakers of languages relying on egocentric reference frames (left/right) on spatial orientation tasks, suggesting that a language's obligatory grammatical categories may train and reinforce certain cognitive habits, rather than merely reflecting pre-existing modes of thought.

Critics caution that such empirical findings should still be interpreted carefully: observing a difference in task performance between speakers of different languages does not necessarily mean the difference originates from the language itself — it may instead be influenced by cultural practices or environmental experience, factors that often covary closely with the use of a particular language and are difficult to fully isolate in cross-linguistic comparative studies.

Passage 2 (Questions 6–10): Substance Transport in the Nephron

The nephron regulates body fluid composition through three coordinated processes: glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption, and secretion. As filtrate flows through the proximal convoluted tubule, most water, glucose, amino acids, and electrolytes are reabsorbed. The filtrate then enters the loop of Henle, whose descending thin limb is highly permeable to water but relatively impermeable to solutes, while the thick ascending limb is the opposite — impermeable to water but actively reabsorbing Na⁺, K⁺, and Cl⁻ — a property essential to establishing the medullary hyperosmotic gradient (the countercurrent multiplier mechanism).

Passage 5 (Questions 21–25): The Cardiac Cycle and Myocardial Physiology

The cardiac cycle consists of systole and diastole, involving cyclical changes in atrial and ventricular pressure and volume that correspond to characteristic heart sounds and ECG waveform features. At the start of ventricular systole, ventricular pressure rises rapidly while volume remains unchanged (isovolumetric contraction), until ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure, the aortic valve opens, and blood is ejected (ejection period); at the start of ventricular diastole, ventricular pressure falls rapidly while volume remains unchanged (isovolumetric relaxation), until ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, the mitral/tricuspid valve opens, and the ventricle fills with blood.

Cardiac muscle contraction follows the sliding filament theory: an action potential triggers Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum; Ca²⁺ binds troponin, relieving tropomyosin's inhibition of actin-myosin interaction; the myosin head then drives a cross-bridge cycle powered by ATP hydrolysis, pulling actin filaments to slide relative to myosin filaments, shortening the sarcomere while the filaments themselves remain the same length.

The ventricle is thus a completely sealed chamber, so myocardial contraction can only manifest as a sharp rise in pressure, with no blood entering or leaving and no volume change.

Passage 6 (Questions 26–30): Population Genetics and Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium describes how, in an idealized large population, in the absence of mutation, migration, non-random mating, and natural selection (and assuming the population is large enough to neglect genetic drift), allele and genotype frequencies remain constant from generation to generation. For a locus with two alleles (A and a, with frequencies p and q respectively, p+q=1), genotype frequencies satisfy: p²(AA) + 2pq(Aa) + q²(aa) = 1. This model is often used as a "null hypothesis," allowing researchers to infer whether a population is experiencing some evolutionary pressure (such as natural selection) by comparing observed genotype frequencies against this theoretical expectation.

Answer: C
Affected individuals have genotype aa, whose frequency is q². Given q²=0.04, so q=√0.04=0.2.

Passage 7 (Questions 31–35): Cell Cycle Checkpoints and Regulation

The eukaryotic cell cycle consists of the G1, S, G2, and M phases in sequence, with progression tightly regulated by cyclin-cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) complexes and monitored at several key "checkpoints" to ensure that the previous phase has been correctly completed before the next phase begins. The G1/S checkpoint (also called the restriction point) ensures the cell has sufficient nutrients, growth signals, and undamaged DNA before entering S phase (DNA replication); the G2/M checkpoint ensures that DNA replication has been completed accurately and without damage before allowing entry into mitosis; the spindle assembly checkpoint ensures that all chromosomes' kinetochores have correctly attached to spindle microtubules before allowing the cell to progress from metaphase to anaphase.

The tumor suppressor protein p53 plays a central role in the DNA damage response: when a cell detects DNA damage, p53 levels rise, inducing expression of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor proteins (such as p21), which arrest the cell cycle at the G1/S checkpoint, allowing time for DNA repair; if the damage is too severe to repair, p53 can also induce apoptosis.

Passage 8 (Questions 36–39): Respiratory Gas Exchange and Acid-Base Regulation

The alveoli are the primary site of gas exchange; O₂ and CO₂ diffuse across the respiratory membrane driven by partial pressure differences between the alveoli and pulmonary capillaries. The respiratory membrane is extremely thin and has a large surface area, favoring rapid gas exchange. Blood transports CO₂ in three forms: about 7% dissolved directly in plasma, about 23% bound to hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin, and about 70% as HCO₃⁻ in plasma (via red blood cell carbonic anhydrase catalyzing CO₂+H₂O→H₂CO₃→H⁺+HCO₃⁻, after which HCO₃⁻ is transported out of the red blood cell into plasma while Cl⁻ moves in to maintain charge balance — a process called the "chloride shift").

Breathing is regulated by the medullary respiratory center; central chemoreceptors primarily respond to changes in cerebrospinal fluid H⁺ concentration (an indirect reflection of blood CO₂ levels), while peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid and aortic bodies) primarily respond to significant drops in blood O₂ partial pressure (and to some extent CO₂ and pH changes). Elevated blood CO₂ is the most important and most sensitive physiological stimulus driving increased ventilation.

About 70% of blood CO₂ is catalyzed by red blood cell carbonic anhydrase into carbonic acid, which dissociates into HCO₃⁻ and H⁺, and is then transported as HCO₃⁻ in plasma — the primary form of CO₂ transport. The remainder is transported as carbaminohemoglobin (about 23%) and dissolved CO₂ (about 7%).

Passage 4 (Questions 16–20): Signal Detection Theory and Sensory Thresholds

Signal detection theory (SDT) provides a framework for understanding how observers distinguish meaningful stimuli ("signal") from background noise, particularly under conditions of uncertainty. Unlike classical threshold theory, which assumes a fixed sensory threshold below which a stimulus is never detected, SDT holds that detection performance depends on both an observer's sensory sensitivity (d', how well the observer can discriminate signal from noise) and their response criterion (a decision-making bias reflecting the observer's threshold for saying "yes, I detected it," which can shift based on motivation, expectation, and the costs and benefits of different types of errors).

SDT categorizes every trial's outcome into one of four types: a "hit" (correctly detecting a signal that is present), a "miss" (failing to detect a signal that is present), a "false alarm" (reporting a signal when none is present), and a "correct rejection" (correctly reporting no signal when none is present). Critically, an observer's response criterion can shift independently of their underlying sensitivity — for example, a radiologist told that missing a tumor is far more costly than a false alarm might adopt a more liberal criterion (saying "yes" more readily), increasing both hit rate and false alarm rate, without any actual change in their ability to perceptually distinguish a tumor from healthy tissue.

Passage 6 (Questions 26–30): Self-Determination Theory and Work Motivation

Self-determination theory (SDT), developed by Deci and Ryan, proposes that human motivation exists on a spectrum from purely externally controlled (extrinsic) to fully internally endorsed (intrinsic), and that psychological well-being and sustained motivation are best supported when three basic psychological needs are satisfied: autonomy (feeling that one's actions are self-endorsed rather than externally coerced), competence (feeling effective and capable in one's activities), and relatedness (feeling connected to and cared for by others). Applied to workplace settings, SDT research suggests that management practices emphasizing autonomy-supportive approaches — such as providing meaningful rationale for tasks, offering choice in how work is completed, and acknowledging employees' perspectives — tend to produce more sustained engagement and better performance on tasks requiring creativity or complex problem-solving, compared to purely controlling approaches that rely heavily on surveillance, deadlines framed as threats, or contingent rewards tied narrowly to specific behaviors. However, SDT researchers note an important qualification: this does not mean external rewards are always counterproductive — rewards that are unexpected, or that are experienced as informational feedback affirming competence rather than as controlling pressure, do not reliably undermine intrinsic motivation and can sometimes support it.

Passage 7 (Questions 31–35): Social Loafing and Groupthink in Group Decision-Making

Group settings can systematically distort both individual effort and collective judgment, through two well-documented but distinct phenomena. Social loafing refers to the tendency for individuals to exert less effort on a task when working collectively than when working individually, a pattern attributed partly to reduced individual accountability (one's contribution is harder to identify and evaluate within a group product) and partly to a diffusion of responsibility for the outcome. Social loafing tends to be more pronounced on tasks where individual contributions are not separately identifiable, and can be substantially reduced when individual accountability is reintroduced — for instance, by making each member's specific contribution visible and separately evaluable.

Groupthink, a related but distinct phenomenon, refers to a mode of group decision-making in which the desire for consensus and cohesion overrides members' willingness to realistically evaluate alternative courses of action, particularly in highly cohesive groups facing high-stakes decisions under time pressure. Symptoms associated with groupthink include an illusion of unanimity (silence being interpreted as agreement), self-censorship (members withholding doubts to avoid disrupting group harmony), and the emergence of self-appointed "mindguards" who shield the group from dissenting information. Unlike social loafing, which primarily concerns reduced individual effort, groupthink concerns a degradation in the quality of group reasoning itself, and can occur even among highly motivated, engaged group members.

Researchers studying interventions to counter groupthink have found that explicitly assigning a "devil's advocate" role — a group member formally tasked with raising objections and alternative viewpoints — can meaningfully improve decision quality, though its effectiveness depends partly on whether the role is perceived as a genuine invitation to dissent or merely a procedural formality.

Passage 8 (Questions 36–40): Attachment Theory and the Strange Situation

Attachment theory, proposed by Bowlby, holds that the emotional bond formed between an infant and its primary caregiver has a profound influence on the infant's subsequent social and emotional development. Ainsworth's "Strange Situation" procedure classifies infant attachment style by observing an infant's behavior across a standardized series of situations involving brief separation from the caregiver, interaction with a stranger, and reunion. Infants are classified as securely attached, avoidantly attached, or ambivalently/resistantly attached, with later researchers adding a disorganized attachment category.

Securely attached infants freely explore their environment while the caregiver is present, show moderate distress upon separation, and quickly seek and accept comfort upon the caregiver's return, settling back to calm. Avoidantly attached infants show relative indifference to both separation and reunion, seeking little contact. Ambivalently attached infants show contradictory behavior at reunion, both seeking contact and resisting comfort. Researchers generally believe that a caregiver's sensitivity and consistency in responding to an infant's signals is an important factor shaping attachment style; early attachment patterns are also thought to be associated to some degree with an individual's interpersonal relationship patterns in adulthood (the "internal working model" hypothesis).
